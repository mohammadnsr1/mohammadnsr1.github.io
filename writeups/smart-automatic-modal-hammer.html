Smart Automatic Modal Hammer

A modal hammer that swings itself with a stepper motor, measures the force it actually produced, and corrects its own aim strike by strike until it lands within a chosen tolerance of the requested force, replacing the trial and error a person needs to hit consistently by hand.

  • With
    Aryan Singh and Keegan J. Moore, University of Nebraska Lincoln
  • Role
    Regression model, control loop, and validation
  • Platform
    Commercial modal hammer on a stepper motor swing, Raspberry Pi, National Instruments DAQ
  • Stack
    MATLAB, Python, closed loop servo control
    • Repository

    Mission

    Understanding how a structure vibrates usually starts with hitting it with a modal hammer and measuring how it responds. Doing that by hand is harder than it sounds. A person cannot reliably reproduce the same strike force at the same location twice, and for nonlinear structures the entire response depends on how hard the structure gets hit, so an inconsistent strike makes the resulting data close to meaningless.

    Commercial automatic hammers exist, but they tend to be expensive, cover a narrow force range, or cannot target a specific force value directly, only reach one through a slow trial and error process. The goal here was a system that hits a requested force, converges quickly, and costs a fraction of the commercial alternative.

    System

    The system pairs a commercial modal hammer with a stepper motor that swings it, controlled by a Raspberry Pi. The motor has a built in encoder and a closed loop servo driver, which corrects for missed steps and shaft misalignment when the hammer impact suddenly loads the motor mid swing. A custom 3D printed mount connects the hammer to the motor shaft.

    Force and structural response are measured through a National Instruments data acquisition system running through MATLAB, which talks to the Raspberry Pi over a serial link so the loop between commanded motor motion and measured force can close automatically, with no person needed between strikes. Total hardware cost came out to roughly 350 dollars, well below what a commercial automatic hammer with a comparable range would cost.

    Predictor corrector control

    The core contribution is a regression model that learns the relationship between the hammer's velocity at impact and the resulting force, trained on hundreds of real strikes the system collected on its own. Given a desired force, the model predicts the motor velocity that should produce it.

    That prediction is not perfect, so the system measures the force it actually produced and applies a successive approximation correction: it adjusts velocity by the same percentage the measured force missed the target by, then strikes again, repeating until the result lands within the user's chosen error tolerance. In practice this converges in six iterations or fewer, usually two or three, compared to the ten or more strikes a skilled person typically needs to get a consistent hit by hand.

    Validation and limitations

    The system was validated on two structures: a linear two story tower, which is what the regression models were trained on, and a model fighter jet wing fitted with a nonlinear vibration absorber, a structure the models had never seen before. Across force levels ranging from tens of newtons up to 3000 newtons, and across two different hammer tip stiffnesses, the system consistently converged to within about 2 to 4 percent of the requested force, including on the unseen wing structure, which is the result that matters most: the approach generalizes rather than memorizing the training structure.

    • Convergence 6 iterations or fewer Usually 2 or 3, versus 10 plus strikes by hand
    • Accuracy 2 to 4 percent Of requested force, across both structures
    • Force range Tens of N to 3000 N Across two hammer tip stiffnesses
    • Hardware cost About $350 Well below commercial automatic hammers

    Repeatability gets worse at high force levels, especially with the softer rubber tip, so high force strikes need their own iteration run rather than reusing motor settings that converged before. The system also has no way to recalibrate its aim if a structure shifts position between strikes, since that would require adding a robotic arm and computer vision on top of an already capable, comparatively cheap system.
